# Chapter 50: The Job Landscape

## 50.1 The Titles

The family tree, in plain words, with the overlaps stated honestly:

| Title | The daily job | Your book covered |
|---|---|---|
| **Data Analyst** | Answer business questions with data: reports, dashboards, ad-hoc analysis, the five working shapes | All of it — this is your target |
| **Business/Reporting Analyst** | Analyst + process: KPIs, weekly packs, stakeholder wrangling | All of it, minus the deepest stats |
| **BI Analyst / Developer** | Dashboards, data models, semantic layers — Power BI/Tableau-centric | Parts II–III, VI strongly |
| **Data Scientist** | Analyst + modelling + experimentation (prediction, causal inference, ML) | Most of it + Ch. 57–58 as the door |
| **Data Engineer** | Build the pipelines and warehouses analysts query | Part V's architecture, then a specialisation |
| **Analytics Engineer** | The modern bridge: dbt, versioned SQL, modelling in the warehouse | Parts III, V, and 57's world |
| **Research/Statistician** | Surveys, experiments, formal inference — SPSS/Stata heartland | Part IV strongly |

The practical reading: junior titles blur — a "Junior Data Analyst" at a retailer and a "Reporting Analyst" at a bank may be the same job; a "Data Scientist (Junior)" may be an analyst job with a grander name (check the *duties*, never the title). Your positioning, straight from this book's stack: **analyst who can automate** — Excel, SQL, statistics, Python, BI — is the profile that fits the widest set of postings in the family.

## 50.2 Decoding Real Postings

Read the *duties* section of a posting as the honest job and the *requirements* as the wishlist (next section). The duties that recur, decoded into this book's chapters:

- "Provide insights and reporting to support decision-making" — the five moves of Chapter 45, on request.
- "Develop and maintain dashboards" — Parts III + VI: model, measures, pages, refresh.
- "Write SQL to extract and transform data" — Parts III + V: the working shapes plus pipelines.
- "Clean and validate data from multiple sources" — the liturgy, in every Part.
- "Present findings to stakeholders" — Chapters 13, 43, 49: the one-pager, the walkthrough, the caveat-first habit.
- "Support A/B testing / experimentation" — Chapter 26's courtroom at industrial scale (57).

The skills-frequency reality check (consistent across market surveys for years): **SQL and Excel appear in the overwhelming majority of analyst postings; a BI tool (Power BI or Tableau) in most; Python in roughly half, rising; statistics fluency valued everywhere but listed least** — because interviewers *test* it rather than list it. This book's stack is ordered the way the market orders itself; that was not an accident.

## 50.3 Where the Jobs Are

- **Industries**: retail and e-commerce (inventory, pricing, customer — Tariro's world, scaled); banking, insurance and fintech (risk, fraud, product analytics — heavy SQL, heavy regulation); health and public sector (research-flavoured, SPSS/Stata heartland — Part IV's tools earn their keep); telecoms (churn, network, revenue assurance); consulting and agencies (many domains fast; client-facing). Match the *domain* to your background when you can — a retail background + retail analytics is a story that interviews itself.
- **Company size, honestly**: **Large companies** — structured graduate/internship pipelines, mentors, narrow first roles ("the SQL person for revenue reporting"), slower decisions. **Small companies and startups** — no pipeline, wider roles ("the data person" — the whole stack of this book), faster learning, less support, and titles that mean whatever the founder means. **Agencies/consultancies** — many clients, many tools, steep communication curve. For a first job, apply widely across all three; the "which is best" question answers itself per offer (Chapter 53's decision table).
- **The entry paths**: internships and graduate programmes (apply even if you think you are "not the type" — the portfolio levels that field); junior/entry postings (the direct route); **adjacent-entry** — the inside move: taking an ops/customer-service/marketing role at a company with a data team and becoming "the person who does the numbers" internally, then transferring — *statistically the most under-rated path into analytics*, and your portfolio makes it deliberate rather than lucky.

## 50.4 Reading Requirements Like a Professional

The requirements list is a *wishlist*, assembled by committee, aspirational by nature. The decoder: **"required" means screened, "nice-to-have" means bonus, and the years-of-experience number is a proxy for competence, not a law** — a posting asking "3+ years, SQL, Python, Power BI" is describing the *work at the level they hope for*; candidates with strong evidence of doing that work get interviews at 1 year, and candidates with 4 undifferentiated years often do not. The professional's response to a requirements list: score yourself honestly against the *duties* (can I do this work? could I learn it in weeks?), then apply if you clear most duties and half the wishlist — and let the portfolio (Chapter 49) carry the argument the CV's years cannot. The one hard filter to respect: genuinely required legal/security clearances, and specific work-authorisation needs in your market; everything else is negotiable with evidence.

## 50.5 Your Region and the Remote World

Hunting from Zimbabwe (this book's home ground) or anywhere outside the big tech corridors, the map is three markets at once: **local** — banks, telecoms (Econet, NetOne, Telecel ecosystems), retailers, NGOs and development agencies (MEAL officer roles are *analyst* roles with a social-sector name — surveys, indicators, reporting: Part IV + the survey project map directly), government statistical agencies; **regional/remote-Africa** — South African and pan-African fintech/retail hiring remotely, in your timezone; **global remote** — the fully-remote junior market is thinner (remote favours mid-level) but real, and grows every year — the portfolio is *more* important there, because it is the only interview you get.

Two honest strategic notes for the region: the NGO/development sector values exactly this book's statistics-and-survey half (SPSS/Stata fluency is a listed requirement in MEAL postings — Chapter 29 was not a detour), and the local salary reality differs from global-remote for identical work — the practical play, for many, is local experience for 12–24 months while building the global-remote portfolio, then choosing deliberately. Chapter 55's freelancing adds a third lane from day one.

## Key Takeaways

- Titles blur; duties are the job: analyst/reporting/BI are your bullseye family, and "analyst who can automate" is the widest-fit profile.
- Posting duties decode to this book's Parts: the stack is market-ordered — SQL and Excel everywhere, BI in most, Python half and rising, statistics tested rather than listed.
- Hunt three company sizes and every entry path — and remember adjacent-entry (join a company, become its data person, transfer): the under-rated route your portfolio supercharges.
- Requirements are wishlists scored by duties; evidence beats years; only clearances and work rights are hard filters.
- Hunt three markets at once (local — where NGO/MEAL roles value the SPSS/Stata half; regional-remote; global-remote), and let the portfolio carry what the CV cannot.
