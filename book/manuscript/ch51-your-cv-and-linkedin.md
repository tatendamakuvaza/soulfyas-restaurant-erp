# Chapter 51: Your CV and LinkedIn

*Part VIII — Getting the Job*

> "A CV is not read; it is skimmed. Write for the skim, and the interview invitation is what survives it."

### In this chapter you will learn

- The analyst's CV, section by section — with the lines that earn interviews.
- Bullet craft: achievements with numbers, not duties with verbs.
- The screening reality: ATS, keywords, and the 30-second human skim.
- LinkedIn as the passive pipeline: the profile that works while you sleep.
- The cover note that gets answered — short, specific, evidence-linked.

## 51.1 The CV, Section by Section

One page (two, absolute maximum, if experience demands it), and the shape:

```text
YOUR NAME                          city · phone · email · LinkedIn · portfolio
          Data Analyst | Excel · SQL · Python · Power BI · Statistics

SUMMARY (3 lines)
  Analyst who automates: [X] projects shipped on real and public data,
  from cleaning to tested findings to dashboards. [Domain] background;
  strong written communication; portfolio: [link]

PROJECTS (the heart — yes, above experience)
  Customer churn analysis — Python, pandas, scipy
    · Found founding-cohort churn at 2× the shop average; named 60
      at-risk customers worth 1.4× mean CLV; designed a save-campaign
      with 8× projected payback
    · Built cohort retention grids and survival curves; 150-word
      case study + reproducible notebook at [link]
  [Pipeline project] — an afternoon of monthly reporting → 20 minutes
  [Public-data wild card] — [the finding, with its number]

EXPERIENCE
  [Role], [Employer], [dates]
    · [achievement with a number — 51.2]
    · ...

EDUCATION & CERTIFICATES
  [Degree/diploma], [institution], [year]
  [Certificates — only ones with exams: e.g. Microsoft PL-300 if taken]

SKILLS (honest, scannable)
  SQL (joins, CTEs, windows) · Excel (pivots, lookups, ToolPak)
  Python (pandas, matplotlib, scipy) · Power BI (model, DAX, publish)
  Statistics (t-tests, chi-square, regression, surveys) · SPSS/Stata
```

The structural argument, for projects above experience: at entry level, your projects *are* your experience — six shipped artefacts beat six months of unnumbered duties, and the recruiter who skims for evidence finds it in the first scroll. When a real analyst job arrives, the sections swap naturally.

## 51.2 Bullet Craft

The formula that converts duties into evidence: **[action] + [what] + [number/measure]**. Before and after, from this book's own work:

- ✗ "Responsible for monthly reports" → ✓ "Rebuilt the monthly reporting pack in Python: an afternoon's manual work became 20 automated minutes, with data-quality checks that abort on broken inputs"
- ✗ "Worked with SQL" → ✓ "Wrote a 10-query monthly analysis pack (joins, CTEs, window functions) reconciled cent-for-cent against the source system"
- ✗ "Skilled in statistics" → ✓ "Closed the shop's Sunday question with a two-sample t-test (t = −18.5, p < 0.001) and attached the confidence interval to the finding"
- ✗ "Created dashboards" → ✓ "Built a Power BI operations dashboard (star schema, 12 DAX measures, drillthrough paths) that weekly planning now runs on"

Every bullet: a verb that did something, an artefact or method, and a number wherever one exists (time saved, rows processed, accuracy, effect size). Numbers are not decoration — they are *evidence*, and after this book you have dozens to choose from. The one law: **never a number or a claim you cannot defend for five minutes under questioning** (Chapter 52 will question exactly these lines).

## 51.3 The Screening Reality

How a CV actually dies: the **ATS** (applicant tracking system) parses it into fields and matches keywords — so mirror the posting's *own words* where honestly true ("Power BI" if the posting says "Power BI", not only "BI tool"; "SQL" spelled exactly so); avoid tables/text-boxes/graphics that parse badly (the pretty-design CV is the ATS's prey); and name the file `Firstname-Lastname-Data-Analyst-CV.pdf`. Then a human — 30 seconds on the first pass: name, current role, top three bullets, portfolio link, done. Both gates are passed by the same craft: *keywords honestly present, evidence in the top third, numbers in every bullet*. (Keyword stuffing — listing tools you cannot use — fails the *interview's* parse; the honesty rules apply to yourself.)

## 51.4 LinkedIn as Pipeline

The profile that works while you sleep, an evening's work: **headline** — "Data Analyst | Excel · SQL · Python · Power BI" (searchable terms, not "aspiring data enthusiast"); **about** — the portfolio's opening paragraph, first person, with the link; **featured** — pin the portfolio repo, the best project's exhibit, the dashboard screenshot; **experience/projects** — the CV's bullets, mirrored; **skills** — the honest list, endorsements gathered from colleagues/classmates (recruiters filter by them); **open-to-work** (visible to recruiters, or publicly if bold — the toggle is yours). Then the habit: one engagement a week — a short post on a project ("built a churn analysis; here's the cohort grid and what surprised me"), a thoughtful comment on someone's analysis post. The algorithm rewards consistency over volume; recruiters *do* read activity feeds; and the discipline costs twenty minutes a week.

## 51.5 The Note That Gets Answered

Applications with a short specific note out-perform naked applies. The four-sentence template:

> Dear [Name] — I'm applying for [exact role]. I fit the [duty X] closely: my [project] did [finding with number], reproducible at [portfolio link]. My background in [domain] adds [one relevant sentence]. I'd welcome a conversation about the team's [something specific from the posting — the dashboard refresh, the experiments].

Specific, evidenced, and *about their posting*, not about your need. Send it to a human where possible (the recruiter named on the posting, or the hiring manager via LinkedIn — one polite message, not a campaign); address hiring managers by name; and never mass-blast identical notes — specificity is the entire mechanism, and its absence reads instantly.

> **From Your Toolkit — one evidence chain:** CV bullet → portfolio project → notebook/report → the code and the log. The chain is what this Part keeps calling *evidence*, and every artefact of it was built by the habits of Parts I–VII: the numbered findings (the report pages), the reproducibility (the packs and pipelines), the honesty labels (the notes' limits lines). The CV is simply the chain's first, shortest link.

## Key Takeaways

- One page, projects above experience at entry level; the summary states the profile ("analyst who automates") and the portfolio link.
- Bullets = action + artefact + number; numbers are evidence; nothing you cannot defend for five minutes.
- ATS: mirror honest keywords, clean layout, named PDF; human skim: evidence in the top third.
- LinkedIn: searchable headline, featured portfolio, mirrored bullets, one honest engagement a week.
- The four-sentence note: specific duty, evidenced fit, portfolio link, one detail from *their* posting — to a human where possible.

## Practice Lab

1. Write the CV from the template — every project bullet carrying a number lifted from your project notes; have a friend skim it for exactly 30 seconds and report what they retain; fix what they missed.
2. The ATS pass: paste your CV into a plain-text reader; check the keywords from your Chapter 50 decoder drill all appear honestly; rename and export the PDF properly.
3. The LinkedIn hour: headline, about, featured (repo + two exhibits), skills, open-to-work decision; then write and schedule (drafts are fine) your first engagement post on the churn project's surprise.
4. The note, written three ways: one application note for a real posting (send it); one cold message to a hiring manager from your Chapter 50 target list (polite, one shot); one referral request to a former colleague or classmate (specific, low-pressure).
5. The chain audit: pick your three strongest CV bullets; click through each to its portfolio artefact; fix any broken link, missing exhibit, or defence-gap you find — the interview will do exactly this, with questions.
