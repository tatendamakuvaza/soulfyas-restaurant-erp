# Chapter 73: Working with Stakeholders — The Question Ladder

*Part XII — The Practitioner's Path: Projects, Clients and the Craft of Delivery*

> "The person closest to the work knows the variable your model is missing."

### In this chapter you will learn

- The stakeholder map: who decides, who uses, who knows, who blocks.
- The question ladder: five questions that turn a request into a brief.
- The listening tour: structured interviews before the data.
- The person closest to the work: front-line knowledge as a data source.
- Managing the room: expectations, weekly notes, and the no-surprises rule.
- Failure modes: the sponsor who vanished and the stakeholder you never met.

## 73.1 Four Kinds of People

Every engagement has four roles, and confusing them is how good analysis dies in bad meetings:

- **The decision-maker** — the one whose behaviour the analysis should change; often not the one who requested it.
- **The user** — who will live with the artefact (the dashboard's daily driver, the model's operator); their workflow is a requirement.
- **The knower** — the front-line staff, the data steward, the twenty-year veteran; your best data source, and the most commonly skipped interview.
- **The blocker** — finance, legal, the union rep (Case 3), whoever holds a veto; found early, a constraint; found late, a casualty.

The map is drawn in week one, on one page, and it names names. **From Your Toolkit — Power BI:** the stakeholder map outlives the meeting, and the dashboard's usage telemetry (who actually opens it, how often) is the map redrawn by reality — Chapter 19's audience discipline, checked against Chapter 43's logs.

## 73.2 The Question Ladder

Five questions, in order, that convert a request into a brief. Each rung produces a sentence of the brief (Chapter 71's one-pager):

1. **"What will you do differently once you know this?"** — the decision. If there is no answer, Chapter 72's refusal clause is on the table; if the answer is "present it to the board", keep climbing.
2. **"What would make you change your mind?"** — the falsifier: the finding that would reverse the decision. It defines the analysis's honest target.
3. **"What did you decide last time, and what did you use?"** — the organisation's real evidence standard, as practised rather than professed.
4. **"Who will disagree, and what will they point at?"** — the objection and its data; the analysis addresses it *in the document*, not in the meeting.
5. **"If I could only give you one number on Friday, what number?"** — the metric. Everything else is scaffolding for it.

The ladder's discipline is the order: decision before data, every time — and the sponsor who cannot climb rung one is a scoping conversation, not a project.

## 73.3 The Listening Tour

Before the data, the people: six to ten structured interviews, thirty minutes, the ladder's questions adapted per role. The tour's outputs, written into the brief:

- The decision, in the decision-maker's own words (Chapter 74 will quote it back to them)
- The known data problems, from the people who live with them (the register that is "mostly right", the outlet that double-enters on Saturdays)
- The history — the last analysis on this question, and why it did or did not stick
- The vocabulary — the organisation's own terms for its metrics, which your artefacts must use or deliberately translate

**From Your Toolkit — SPSS:** the interview notes are survey data you gathered by hand — code them like it (thematic buckets, counts, the dissenting view preserved), and the brief's "what we heard" section writes itself with evidence instead of impressions.

## 73.4 The Person Closest to the Work

Case 3's union rep is the part's patron saint: one sentence about loading order — the constraint the optimiser missed, the variable that reversed two routes. The discipline: **front-line knowledge is data**, gathered and cited like data. The till operator knows the 16:00 rush's true shape; the nurse knows which register is fiction; the driver knows which road the map does not believe. Interview them early, quote them in the analysis (with permission), and check the model's behaviour against their account — when the model and the front line disagree, one of them is teaching you something, and it is usually the model being taught.

## 73.5 Managing the Room

- **The weekly note** — five lines every Friday: what we did, what we found, what is next, what we need, decisions pending. The no-surprises rule's mechanism: the sponsor never learns a headline in the read-out they have not seen in a note.
- **Expectations as commitments** — "the draft is Thursday 10:00" is a promise with a name on it; slipped dates are announced before they pass, never after.
- **Disagreement engineered early** — the ladder's rung four finds the objector; bring them the interim finding before the final, so the objection arrives while it can improve the work instead of ambushing it.
- **The meeting is not the medium** — the artefact carries the analysis; the meeting carries the decision. Never present findings for the first time in the meeting where they must be decided (the no-surprises rule again, from the other side).

## 73.6 Failure Modes

- **The sponsor who vanished** — no gate signatures, no weekly reads; the project ends with a full report and an empty room. The gates exist to make the absence visible in week two, not week twelve.
- **The stakeholder you never met** — the knower or blocker skipped for speed; Case 3's route plan, signed off by everyone except the person who knew the loading rule.
- **The echo chamber** — interviews with the leadership team only; the front line's reality never enters the brief, and the analysis beautifully answers the question as misunderstood.
- **Rung-skipping** — data before decision: the ladder climbed from the bottom, producing a technically superb answer to the wrong question.

> **Teaching Tip — The silent stakeholder:** run a stakeholder role-play with one rule — one participant receives a private brief with a critical fact (the loading order, the broken register) and may not volunteer it; they only answer questions asked. Groups that interview poorly ship a flawed plan and feel the cost; groups that ask "what does your day actually look like?" get the fact and win. The exercise teaches the ladder better than the ladder does.

## Key Takeaways

- Map four roles — decision-maker, user, knower, blocker — with names, in week one.
- The question ladder turns requests into briefs: decision, falsifier, precedent, objection, the one number.
- The listening tour is data collection: code it, cite it, and use the organisation's own vocabulary.
- The person closest to the work knows the missing variable; treat front-line knowledge as data.
- The weekly note makes no-surprises mechanical, and disagreements are engineered to arrive early.

## Practice Lab

1. Draw the stakeholder map for Case 5 (the co-operative evaluation): decision-maker, users, knowers, blockers — and note which of them the case's analyst actually needed.
2. Run the question ladder on a real request in your organisation; write the five brief-sentences it produces, and note where the climbing stalled.
3. Conduct three listening-tour interviews (colleagues playing roles, or real ones); code the notes into themes and write the "what we heard" section with counts.
4. The front-line check: take a live model or dashboard and validate it against a front-line account; document every disagreement and which party was right.
5. Draft the weekly note for a project you know (or Case 3, week two): five lines, no surprises, one pending decision.
6. Run the silent-stakeholder role-play; write the two interview questions that would have extracted the private fact, and keep them for the rest of your career.
