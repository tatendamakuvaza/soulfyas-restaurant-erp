# Chapter 56: The Career Ladder

*Part VIII — Getting the Job*

> "The ladder is not a ladder. It is a widening of the question — from 'what did the data say?' to 'what should we ask?' to 'what should we never have done?'"

### In this chapter you will learn

- The stages: junior → analyst → senior → lead/principal — what actually changes.
- The two ladders: management vs craft (IC), and choosing honestly.
- Specialising: the domains and niches, and when to commit.
- Staying current cheaply: the filter that beats the feed.
- The compounding career: what the second decade looks like from the first.

## 56.1 The Stages

What changes at each step — the honest version, from the job's inside:

- **Junior analyst (year 0–1)**: given questions, told the data. Success = trustworthy execution: the reconciliation habit, shipped work, stakeholders who say "easy to work with". Your 90-day chapter is the whole syllabus.
- **Analyst (1–3)**: given *goals*, find the questions. Success = ownership: you scoped the engagement yourself, pushed back on the fuzzy brief, chose the method, defended the caveat in the meeting. The Chapter 45 anatomy is now yours to run unsupervised.
- **Senior (3–6)**: given *problems*, find the goals. Success = judgement: you know which of the five requests matter; your estimates are trusted before the data arrives; you design the experiments, not just run them; juniors get better because of you. The tell that you are here: people ask you "*should* we look at this?" rather than "*can* you?"
- **Lead/Principal (6+)**: given *directions*, find the problems. Two forms — the **manager's** path (people, hiring, priorities, the team's output as your product) and the **principal's** path (the organisation's hardest analytical problems, methodology, the standard others follow). The compensation flattens between them; the work is simply different, and the choice is about *you*, not status.

The mechanics of moving up, identical at every step: **scope you can prove** (the self-review document of Chapter 54, kept quarterly, becomes the promotion case — "here is what I now own that I didn't"), **a successor-shaped gap** (you get promoted into bigger work when your current work survives without you — the runbooks and handovers pay off here, literally), and **sponsorship** (a senior person who has *seen* your work argue for it in the room you are not in — which is why credit-sharing and stakeholder craft are career skills, not manners).

## 56.2 Two Ladders, One Honest Choice

Somewhere around senior, the paths fork, and pretending they don't is how careers stall: **management** — people, plans, hiring, the meeting-heavy calendar; your output becomes the team's output, and the craft skills convert into coaching and judgement calls. **Craft (IC)** — deeper specialisation, the hardest problems, methodology ownership; your output remains the analysis, at higher stakes and wider scope. Both are careers, both pay, and the honest test is where your energy goes: a day of one-on-ones and roadmaps that ends with you energised → management; a day of stakeholder wrangling that ends with you longing for the notebook → craft. (The test is not which you are *good* at — by senior you are good at both, that is what senior means.) And the choice is reversible for a while, then hardens; try the adjacent path *inside* your role first (mentor a junior for a quarter; lead one project) before you bet a title on it.

## 56.3 Specialising

The generalist stack of this book is the *entry* ticket; somewhere in years 2–5, a specialisation compounds faster than breadth. The honest map of niches — each a combination of *domain* (retail, health, finance, development/NGO, telecoms) and *function* (pricing, churn/retention, experimentation/A-B, fraud/risk, supply chain, survey research, data visualisation, analytics engineering):

- Choose by **accumulated advantage**: the domain you keep ending up in, the stakeholder questions you answer best, the projects in your portfolio that generated the follow-on work. Specialisation chosen from evidence beats one chosen from lists — it is a Chapter 28 argument about yourself (mechanism, not vibes).
- The **development-sector niche** deserves a flag for this book's readers: MEAL (monitoring, evaluation, accountability, learning) is a career *and* a consultancy market where Part IV's statistics, the survey craft, SPSS/Stata, and honest reporting against logframes are the daily trade — and where a freelancer-to-consultant ladder (Chapter 55's practice, grown) is a well-worn path.
- **When NOT to specialise**: before you have seen three domains. Early over-commitment (the "I only do churn in fintech" of year one) is betting the career on one hand you haven't been dealt.

## 56.4 Staying Current, Cheaply

The field's firehose is a manufactured anxiety; the professional's filter is small and stable: **one weekly newsletter** (a general data one — e.g. a widely read analytics or data-engineering digest — not five), **one community** (a local analytics meetup or an online forum where you *answer* as well as ask — answering is where learning compounds), **the release notes of your actual stack** (Power BI monthly updates, pandas releases, your warehouse's changelog — thirty minutes a month, because the tools you use daily are the ones whose changes matter), and **one deliberate annual stretch** (a new tool, a small course, a conference — chosen in December against the year's gap list, not joined in January from guilt). Everything else — the daily posts, the "you're behind" content economy — is someone else's funnel; the Chapter 2 questions, applied to the feed itself: *says who, compared to what, what's missing, is it even true?*

## 56.5 The Compounding Career

What the second decade looks like, so the first is spent well: skills compound — the reconciliation habit that made you trustworthy as a junior makes your estimates trusted as a senior; the runbooks that got you promoted become the data culture you eventually lead. Reputation compounds — the analyst who says "let me check the data" in 2026 is the consultant whose word settles arguments in 2032. And the portfolio compounds — Chapter 49's shelf, kept alive, becomes the speaker bio, the consulting credential, the book of case studies (some analysts literally publish theirs; you have, in a sense, read one). The one-sentence version of this entire chapter, and of this Part: **the career is a dataset with a long tail — collect honestly, reconcile often, and let the compounding do what compounding does.**

> **From Your Toolkit — the long game:** the ladder's stages are the anatomy's moves at increasing scope (execute → own → judge → direct); the two ladders are the tool-choice chapter (Ch. 35's audience rule: management serves the team, craft serves the question); specialisation is a regression on your own history; staying current is the first-hour discipline applied to the field. This book has one method; the career is where you get to run it for decades.

## Key Takeaways

- The stages widen the question: juniors execute, analysts own, seniors judge, leads direct — and promotion mechanics are provable scope, a successor-shaped gap, and sponsorship.
- The fork is real (management vs craft/IC); test adjacent inside the role before betting a title; choose by where energy goes, not talent.
- Specialise from accumulated advantage around years 2–5 (domain × function); MEAL is a real ladder for this book's stack; not before three domains seen.
- Current cheaply: one newsletter, one community (where you answer), your stack's release notes, one deliberate annual stretch — the feed is a funnel; audit it.
- The career compounds like a long-tail dataset: habits → reputation → portfolio → culture; collect honestly, reconcile often.

## Practice Lab

1. The stage map: write your current stage and its success definition in your own words, then the two behaviours that would evidence the *next* stage within six months — the promotion case, started early.
2. The fork test, taken honestly: log your energy for one week (days that ended energised vs drained, and what those days contained); write the one-paragraph verdict; note the adjacent-path experiment you will run first.
3. The specialisation regression: list every project you have touched (this book's and life's); tally domain and function; write the "accumulated advantage" reading — what does the evidence say you keep becoming?
4. The current-stack budget: choose your one newsletter, one community, and stack release-notes half-hour now; calendar the annual stretch decision for December; unsubscribe from the rest this week — the filter, installed.
5. The decade letter: write one page to yourself at year ten — what the compounding should have produced; seal it in the portfolio repo (a file named `letter-to-year-ten.md`); the commit message is part of the artefact.
