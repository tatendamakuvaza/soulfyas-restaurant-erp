# Chapter 55: Freelancing and Side Income

*Part VIII — Getting the Job*

> "A freelance analyst is what you become the moment someone pays you for one project. The craft is making that moment deliberate, priced, and repeatable."

### In this chapter you will learn

- The honest economics of analyst freelancing — what sells, what doesn't.
- Finding the first three clients: the market that already surrounds you.
- Pricing small jobs: the three models, and the beginner's mistake with each.
- Delivering like a pro: scope, contracts-lite, and the handover.
- Building the side practice without wrecking the day job (or the ethics).

## 55.1 What Actually Sells

The honest market scan: strangers do not buy "data analysis" (too abstract); they buy **finished business outcomes that happen to be analytical**. What sells at small scale, in rough order of demand:

- **The spreadsheet rescue** — the broken workbook, the slow report, the VLOOKUP that stopped matching after someone inserted a column. (Part II, productised: diagnosis, rebuild, a walkthrough for the owner.)
- **The monthly-report automation** — exactly Project 3: "your three hours every month become ten minutes." Easiest sale in the market because the ROI is arithmetic.
- **The first dashboard** — small shops, NGOs, school associations: one Power BI/Metabase page on data they already collect. Part VI, miniaturised.
- **The survey and its analysis** — NGOs and researchers needing instruments, field protocol, and the stats: Part IV + Project 5, and in development-sector work this is a *listed procurement category* (MEAL consultancies).
- **The one-question analysis** — "should we stock X? did the price change hurt us? which branch is lying?" — Project-style scoped engagements on the client's own data.

What does *not* sell at small scale: machine learning, "insights", anything whose value you cannot state as a sentence with a number in it. If you cannot tell the client what they will *have* at the end that they lack now, it is not a product.

## 55.2 The First Three Clients

You do not find the first clients; you *notice* them — they are the market that already surrounds you:

1. **Your current or former workplace** (if employed): the adjacent departments with spreadsheet pain. The internal side-practice: solve one visible problem in your own time on terms you negotiate (and clear with your employer — see 55.5).
2. **Your network's businesses**: the cousin's shop, the church's accounts, the friend's transport company, the NGO where someone you know volunteers. Your first paying engagement is one conversation away and probably already complains about its numbers. (Tariro's Grocery began exactly here — a relative's shop, a first free-ish project, a case study.)
3. **Small local orgs and associations**: schools (fees analysis), clinics (patient flows), farmer's cooperatives, savings clubs, church networks — organisations with real data and no analyst, reachable by one genuine conversation, priced accessibly (55.3).

Then the platforms — Upwork/Fiverr and their kin — as the *fourth* channel, with honest expectations: global competition on price, slow first jobs, but a real training ground for scoping and communication once you have three reviews. The portfolio (Chapter 49) is your profile there; lead with the wild-card real-data project.

The **first engagement, structured**: offer it deliberately cheap or free *by design, not by accident* — "I'll do this one for [small fee/free] because I'm building my practice in this niche; in exchange, may I use the (anonymised) case study and would you refer me if it's good?" — that is a marketing spend with a defined return, and it converts satisfied clients into the referral engine that becomes your pipeline.

## 55.3 Pricing

Three models, the beginner mistake with each, and the fixes:

- **Hourly** ("$X/hour"): the mistake — beginners price at *low* hourly to win, then discover that getting good makes each job pay *less* (faster = fewer hours). The fix: hourly only for open-ended diagnosis ("exploratory, capped at N hours"), priced at your day-job equivalent × 1.5 minimum (self-employed carries its own costs), and always with a cap stated.
- **Fixed per deliverable** ("$300 for the automated monthly report"): the mistake — scoping by optimism ("it's just a pivot!") and eating the overrun. The fix: fixed price *only after* a paid or free scoping look at the actual data (the first hour of Chapter 44 *is* the scoping product), with the deliverable written as a checklist and a round of revisions counted.
- **Value/retainer** ("$80/month: the dashboard kept fresh, questions answered, quarterly review"): the mistake — never offering it, leaving recurring value on the table. The fix: propose the retainer at handover of every successful project ("what happens next month when the data changes?") — recurring revenue is the freelancer's compounding asset, exactly like Chapter 62's career habit.

The universal beginner error: **under-pricing to win**, which attracts the worst clients (price-shoppers churn and complain), devalues the work, and sets an anchor you will spend a year raising. The counter-intuitive truth of small-market freelancing: at accessible-but-confident prices, with a portfolio and a scoping conversation, you *close more* than at desperation prices — because the price signals the seriousness. Start at the price that makes you slightly uncomfortable; raise it every third engagement.

## 55.4 Delivering Like a Pro

The craft of the small engagement, all of it this book's habits compressed:

- **The one-page scope** — not a contract; a plain agreement: the question, the deliverable checklist, the timeline, the price, what is *not* included ("data cleaning beyond X hours is separate"), and the revision policy. Both parties keep a copy. This single page prevents 90% of freelance misery (the other 10% is scope creep the client genuinely didn't intend — handled by "happy to; that's outside our scope page; I'll send the add-on price today").
- **The working discipline** — the anatomy (Ch. 45) runs every engagement: question written, loading checks, the analysis, caveats. Small clients get *the same rigour* as the book's projects — that is precisely why they keep you.
- **The handover** — deliver the artefacts *plus the runbook* (how to refresh, what the checks mean, what to do when something breaks), a 30-minute walkthrough (Chapter 43's five-minute format, expanded), and the retainer offer. The client who can run it without you and *still* keeps you — that is the relationship worth building.
- **The paper trail** — an invoice for every engagement (even the free one, marked "no charge — case-study arrangement"), a folder per client, a simple ledger (engagement, dates, fee, hours, margin, referral source). The ledger is your pricing data: after six engagements, *your own numbers* tell you your real hourly rate and which work earns it — the analytics habit, applied to the practice itself.

## 55.5 The Ethics and the Day Job

The two clearances that keep the side practice safe: **your employment terms** — many contracts require disclosure or limit outside work; ask, in writing, before the first paid engagement; the conversation is normal and the alternative is a career hazard. And **your clients' data** — the Chapter 47 conscience, now contractual: minimum data (collect only what the engagement needs), explicit permission for any case study (anonymise to the bone), no client data on personal unsecured devices, and no work you cannot defend (if a client wants you to "find the numbers that justify the decision" — the request will come — that is a refusal, politely: "I can find what the data says; I can't decide what it says first"). The freelancer's brand *is* the honesty rules; there is no employer's reputation to hide behind, only yours.

> **From Your Toolkit — the practice is the book, sold:** every freelance product in this chapter is a Part of this book wearing a price tag: the spreadsheet rescue (II), the query pack (III), the tested finding (IV), the automation (V/37), the dashboard (VI), the survey (47). You are not learning a new trade — you are packaging the one you have, and the packaging (scoping page, ledger, handover runbook, retainer) is the only genuinely new craft here.

## Key Takeaways

- Sell outcomes, not analysis: rescues, automations, first dashboards, surveys, one-question projects — if the value has no sentence with a number, it has no market.
- First clients are your surroundings: workplace (cleared), network's businesses, local orgs — then platforms as channel four; structure the first engagement as deliberate marketing with a case-study return.
- Price: hourly (capped, ×1.5) for diagnosis; fixed only after scoping; always offer the retainer at handover — and never under-price; start slightly uncomfortable, raise every third job.
- Deliver with the one-page scope, the full rigour, the handover runbook, and the ledger — your own numbers become your pricing analytics.
- Clear the day-job terms in writing; take minimum data; anonymise case studies; refuse the "find me the right numbers" request — the honesty rules are the brand.

## Practice Lab

1. The product list: write your three sellable products as one-paragraph offers (outcome, deliverable checklist, timeline band, price band) — the spreadsheet rescue, the automation, the dashboard; these paragraphs become your profiles and your pitch.
2. The market scan: list ten real prospects in your surroundings (workplace-adjacent, network, local orgs), each with the product that fits and the one person you would talk to; mark the three warmest.
3. The scope page, drafted: take your strongest product and write its one-page scope agreement template — question, checklist, exclusions, revisions, price blank; keep it; you will use it.
4. The pricing rehearsal: price your automation product three ways (hourly capped, fixed post-scope, retainer) with your real numbers from the project ledger (hours it took you, honestly); note which model you would defend and why.
5. The first pitch, made: approach one warm prospect this week with a specific offer ("I can turn your monthly [X] into a ten-minute job — a look at the data is free; the build is [price]"); log the outcome whatever it is — the pipeline metric, started.
