# Chapter 71: The Anatomy of an Analytics Project

*Part XII — The Practitioner's Path: Projects, Clients and the Craft of Delivery*

> "Projects fail at the beginning, at the middle, and at the end — in that order of preventability."

### In this chapter you will learn

- The engagement arc: scope, plan, analyse, deliver — and the gate between each.
- The analysis plan: one page that prevents a month of drift.
- The stranger test: the quality gate every artefact passes before it ships.
- Mid-project reviews: the structured checkpoint that catches drift early.
- The deliverable ladder: memo, deck, dashboard, handover — choosing the right rung.
- Failure modes: scope creep, the endless exploration, and the vanishing analyst.

## 71.1 The Arc

Every analytics engagement — internal or consulting, two weeks or two quarters — runs the same four phases, and the phases are separated by **gates**: explicit moments where the work is reviewed against its plan before it continues.

| Phase | Output | Gate to next phase |
|---|---|---|
| Scope | The brief: question, decision, data, owner, date | Sponsor signs the one-pager |
| Plan | The analysis plan: methods, data pulls, artefacts, timeline | Plan reviewed against the brief |
| Analyse | Findings, models, checked assumptions | Mid-project reviews at 1/3 and 2/3 |
| Deliver | Memo/deck/dashboard, handover, measurement plan | Stranger test + sponsor read-out |

The arc's discipline is that **the gates are real**: skipping the scope gate is how three-week projects become three-month explorations; skipping the review gates is how projects deliver a beautiful answer to a question nobody asked.

## 71.2 The Analysis Plan

One page, written before the analysis, containing:

- **The question and the decision it serves** (Chapter 1's discipline, in writing)
- **The metric and its baseline** — what number, compared to what
- **Data sources and known gaps** — the data card, referenced
- **Methods, in order** — and the order is the argument: descriptive first, causal where the question demands, models where they pay
- **Artefacts and owners** — memo, dashboard, model card; who writes each
- **Timeline with review dates** — the mid-project reviews booked at the start, not found at the end

The plan's value is not prescience — plans change — but **drift detection**: when week three's work does not serve the plan, either the work or the plan is wrong, and the plan makes the conflict visible. Appendix J's five cases all include their analysis plans for exactly this reason: the plan is where the evaluator looks first.

## 71.3 The Stranger Test

The quality gate for every artefact: **hand it to a stranger — someone with the relevant job but no context — and ask them to state the decision it recommends and why.** If they cannot, in under a minute for the executive summary, the artefact is not done. The test catches the practitioner's occupational disease: the curse of knowledge, where the context in your head substitutes for the context on the page. Run it before the sponsor sees anything; the stranger's confusion is cheap, the sponsor's is expensive.

## 71.4 Mid-Project Reviews

At roughly one-third and two-thirds of the timeline, a structured checkpoint (the same format as Chapter 50's studio reviews, professional edition):

1. **Two strengths** — what is working and should be protected.
2. **Two risks** — what could sink the project, stated with evidence.
3. **One recommendation** — the single change that most improves the outcome.

The review's rule: the analyst presents *work in progress, including the ugly parts* — because the review's entire value is catching drift while it is still cheap. Receiving critique without defending (Chapter 50's hardest skill) is a professional requirement here, not a classroom exercise: the review is against the project, not the person.

## 71.5 The Deliverable Ladder

Chapter 49's product ladder, delivery-side: the **memo** (the default — Chapter 74's structures), the **deck** (when the room is the medium), the **dashboard** (when the question repeats), the **handover** (when the artefact must live without you: code, docs, the analysis log, the owner named). The delivery failure is stopping at the wrong rung — a dashboard for a one-time decision, a memo for a weekly ritual — and the discipline is Chapter 49's question: *how many people, how often, making which decision?*

**From Your Toolkit — Excel:** the analysis plan, the review schedule, and the deliverable checklist run happily in a spreadsheet — and they should, because the project's operational artifacts must live where the team works, not in a tool the project outlives.

## 71.6 Failure Modes

- **Scope creep** — the brief that quietly grows ("while you're in there..."); the gate is the sponsor's signature on the one-pager, and every addition is a re-scope conversation, not a favour.
- **The endless exploration** — "one more data source" for six weeks; the plan's timeline is the alarm, and the review is where it rings.
- **The vanishing analyst** — heads-down for a month, emerging with a 60-page report; the no-surprises rule (Chapter 74) exists because silence reads as risk.
- **The unfinished finish** — findings delivered, nothing measured; every project ends with its measurement plan: what will be watched, by whom, for how long, to learn whether the recommendation worked.

> **Teaching Tip — The plan autopsy:** give students a failed project (any real one, names removed, or Appendix J Case 3's intern) and have them write the analysis plan that would have prevented the failure. Then — the twist — have them write the plan's drift log: the three moments the real project departed from it. Students learn that plans fail by drift, not by being wrong, and that the gate's job is to make drift loud.

## Key Takeaways

- Four phases, real gates: scope, plan, analyse, deliver — with the sponsor's signature between each.
- The one-page analysis plan is drift detection: when work and plan diverge, one of them is wrong, loudly.
- The stranger test gates every artefact: a stranger states the decision in a minute or it is not done.
- Reviews at one-third and two-thirds, work-in-progress included; critique without defence is the professional baseline.
- End every project with its measurement plan — the recommendation is a hypothesis until it is watched.

## Practice Lab

1. Write the analysis plan for Case 1 (the promotion post-mortem): question, metric, baseline, methods in order, artefacts, timeline, review dates — one page.
2. Run the stranger test on your last deliverable; log every question the stranger needed to ask — each one is context that was in your head instead of on the page.
3. Run a mid-project review on a live piece of work (yours or a classmate's): two strengths, two risks, one recommendation, and the drift log against the plan.
4. The deliverable ladder decision: take three recent requests in your organisation and place each on the ladder rung it deserved — then note what was actually delivered and what the gap cost.
5. The scope-creep rehearsal: script the conversation when the sponsor adds "just one more cut" at week two — practice saying the re-scope sentence out loud until it is comfortable.
6. Write the measurement plan for your capstone: what gets watched, by whom, for how long, and what result would prove the recommendation wrong.
