# Chapter 53: Cases and Behaviour — the Offer

*Part VIII — Getting the Job*

> "The technical round proves you can analyse. The case and behaviour rounds prove they can put you in a room with their stakeholders — and the negotiation proves you can be in one with them."

### In this chapter you will learn

- The case interview: the metric-decomposition framework that fits in your pocket.
- Behavioural rounds: STAR stories from your own ledger, told in 90 seconds.
- The take-home presentation: defending your analysis to a panel.
- Questions *you* ask — the underrated scoring round.
- Negotiation, politely: the script, the silence, and the decision table.

## 53.1 The Case Interview

The analyst case ("engagement is down 15% — what do you do?") is graded on *structure*, and the structure is Chapter 45's anatomy with a clock. The pocket framework — five moves, spoken aloud:

1. **Clarify the metric** — "Engagement — how is it defined here, and who defines it? Over what baseline?" (Never accept a fuzzy noun; the clarification *is* the first analytical act.)
2. **Segment the fall** — "I'd break the 15% down: by channel, by user group, by platform, new vs returning. A 15% average is usually 0% somewhere and −40% somewhere else." (Decomposition — the pivots of Part II, the GROUP BYs of Part III, as instinct.)
3. **Data first** — "Before believing the story, I'd check the data: tracking changes, missing days, a definition shift. Twice in my work, the 'drop' was a measurement artefact." (Says the candidate this book built — and it lands.)
4. **Hypothesise and test** — "Say mobile-new-users are the whole fall: I'd look for what changed — release, pricing, a competitor, seasonality — and distinguish coincidence, confounding, cause." (Chapter 28's three explanations, named.)
5. **Recommend with measurement** — "The fix depends on the segment; I'd propose the cheapest reversible action and define the metric that decides in two weeks." (Recommendation + kill-line, the Chapter 13 discipline.)

Cases are *conversations*: the interviewer feeds facts as you probe ("actually, desktop is up") — and the grading favours adaptation over a recited ladder. If a hypothesis dies, say so and pivot: "then seasonality is out; let me look at the release calendar." The fatal case errors are two: silence (thinking invisibly) and sureness (leaping to cause without the data check).

## 53.2 Behavioural Rounds: Your Ledger, Spoken

"Tell me about a time you..." — and the answer is already written, in your project ledger. **STAR** in 90 seconds: **S**ituation (10s), **T**ask (10s), **A**ction (40s — the majority, and the only part that is about you), **R**esult (30s — with the number). From the six projects, prepare six stories, mapped to the six questions every behavioural round asks:

| The question | Your story (examples from this book's ledger) |
|---|---|
| "…a time you found something others missed" | The say/do gap (Project 5): 41% intent reframed to 15–20% by joining survey to behaviour |
| "…you made a mistake / data was wrong" | The fan-out or the missing weeks: totals doubled, caught by reconciliation, never repeated |
| "…you had to explain something complex" | The five-minute dashboard walkthrough to a non-analyst (Ch. 43); the Sunday verdict in shop-owner sentences |
| "…you disagreed with a stakeholder" | The loyalty-scheme caveat: presenting the association-not-causation limit to an owner who wanted "proof" |
| "…you worked to a deadline" | The 48-hour take-home: scoped to the strongest finding, shipped the honest limits |
| "…you improved a process" | An afternoon became twenty minutes (Project 3): the automation story with its before/after |

Tell them honestly, including the limits — "the finding was real but the cause was unknown, so we designed a trial" is a *stronger* story than a clean fake, because it is the shape of real work and interviewers know it. Rehearse aloud, timed, once each — polished but not robotic; the 40-second action segment should carry the *decisions* ("I chose a paired test because the same items were measured twice"), because decisions are what they are buying.

## 53.3 The Take-Home Presentation

The final round of many processes: your take-home, presented (10 minutes + questions) to a panel. The format that wins is Chapter 43's walkthrough, applied to their data: **headline first** (the finding, with its number, in the first 30 seconds); *then* the method in three steps (question, what the data needed, the analysis); *then* the finding properly (evidence, effect size, the chart that carries it); *then* limitations and next steps, offered voluntarily; then questions. The Q&A is where offers are won: slow down, answer the question asked, say "I don't know — here's how I'd find out" where true (the most under-rated sentence in interviewing), and treat the hostile question ("isn't this just seasonality?") as the gift it is — a chance to show the three explanations and the data check *in action*: "Good challenge — that's exactly the confounder I checked second; here's the seasonal split." Panels remember composure under challenge longer than any slide.

## 53.4 The Questions You Ask

"Any questions for us?" is a scored round, not a courtesy. The questions that score — because they are *analyst questions*:

- "What does the team's data stack look like end-to-end — from source to the dashboards stakeholders actually open?"
- "How do decisions actually get made here — has an analysis changed a decision recently? What happened?" (You are interviewing their data culture; a team that cannot name a recent analysis-driven decision will teach you bad habits.)
- "What does success look like for this role in the first 90 days?" (Chapter 54 is your answer script, prepared.)
- "How is data quality handled — is there a checking culture, or do analysts discover surprises in production?"

Ask two or three, genuinely (you *are* gathering decision data — Chapter 53's whole subject applied to your own career). The questions you do not ask: salary (that round has its own moment, next section), "what does the company do" (you knew before applying), and anything the interviewer already answered.

## 53.5 The Offer and the Negotiation

The moment an offer exists, the power balance shifts: they have decided they want you; everything from here is *expected* to be discussed. The polite script, in three moves:

1. **Thank, and buy time** — "Thank you — I'm genuinely excited about this. I'd like two or three days to review the full package. Could you send the complete terms in writing?" (Written terms matter: bonuses, review timing, leave, study support, remote days — the full package, not the headline salary.)
2. **The counter, once, evidence-backed** — "Based on [market range for the role — Glassdoor/Payscale/local peers] and the portfolio evidence I bring [automation from day one], I was hoping for [number]. Is there flexibility?" Then **stop talking** — the silence is the negotiation; whoever fills it first concedes ground. One counter, polite and specific; not a duel.
3. **The decision table** — when offers or options compete, score them like an analyst (you are one): scope of learning (which tools/domain will this role grow?), mentorship (is there a senior analyst to learn from?), data culture (the "changed a decision" answer), comp (total, including growth trajectory), stability and location. Weight the rows by *your* current priority — junior years should weight learning heavily — and let the table decide, then honour it (the Chapter 2 discipline, applied to yourself: the analysis is finished when the decision is made).

A declined negotiation is not a lost offer (in the overwhelming majority of professional processes, a polite counter changes the number or changes nothing); an accepted-first-number is a permanent anchor. And the rejections that will still come — they come to everyone, at every level — are data for the pipeline, not verdicts on your worth: log them (stage, what was asked, what you'd strengthen), adjust, continue. The hunt is a funnel, and funnels are measured, not mourned.

> **From Your Toolkit — the interview is the analysis:** case = the five moves; behaviour = the ledger with numbers; presentation = the walkthrough; your questions = the data-culture audit; the offer = the decision table. You are not performing a different skill in interviews — you are *running the discipline of this book on yourself as the dataset*. That reframe (and it is fully true) is the calm that wins rooms.

## Key Takeaways

- The case framework: clarify the metric → segment the fall → data check first → hypothesise with the three explanations → recommend with a kill-line; talk, adapt, and let hypotheses die loudly.
- Six STAR stories from your project ledger, 90 seconds each, action-majority, numbers in results, limits told honestly.
- Take-home presentations: headline in 30 seconds, method in three steps, limits volunteered, "I don't know — here's how I'd find out" as a strength.
- Your questions are scored: ask about the stack, the decision culture, and the 90-day success definition — you are auditing them.
- Negotiate politely once with market evidence, then silence; decide with a weighted table; log rejections as funnel data.

## Practice Lab

1. The case, run twice: "engagement down 15%" spoken to a camera (10 min); then a friend plays interviewer feeding you a twist every two minutes; review both recordings against the five moves.
2. The six stories, drafted and timed: write each STAR in under 120 words; speak each in 90 seconds; polish the action segment to carry decisions; store in `interview-stories.md` with the numbers bolded.
3. The hostile-question gym: brainstorm the ten hardest questions your projects invite ("why not a regression here?", "isn't this just seasonality?", "wouldn't the real fix be X?"); answer each in three sentences; rehearse the composure, not just the words.
4. The mock offer: role-play the script with a friend playing a friendly-but-firm recruiter (counter, silence, decision); write your real decision table now — weights chosen — so the real moment has a prepared analyst in it.
5. The funnel log, started: every application gets a row (date, company, stage, what was asked, lesson); the metrics of your own hunt — applications → screens → technicals → finals → offers — are conversion rates you can improve deliberately, which is the exact skill this book taught.
