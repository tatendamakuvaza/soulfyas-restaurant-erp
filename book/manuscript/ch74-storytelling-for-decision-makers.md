# Chapter 74: Storytelling for Decision-Makers

*Part XII — The Practitioner's Path: Projects, Clients and the Craft of Delivery*

> "The analysis is done when the decision is made, not when the deck is polished."

### In this chapter you will learn

- Three structures that carry any finding: the pyramid, the arc, and the memo.
- The executive summary: the one paragraph that survives if nothing else does.
- Charts in the room: one message per slide, stated as its title.
- The hostile-question protocol: preparing for the three hardest questions.
- The no-surprises rule: pre-briefing as a delivery discipline.
- Failure modes: the deck as artefact, the buried lede, and the defensive answer.

## 74.1 Three Structures

Every finding this book has produced — a model, a forecast, a case — ships in one of three shapes:

- **The pyramid (Minto)** — the answer first, then the three reasons, then the evidence for each. The structure of the executive read-out: the decision-maker can stop at any level and leave informed. *Recommendation → reasons → evidence*, never the reverse.
- **The arc** — context, complication, resolution. The structure of the narrative memo and the presentation: the world as it was, what changed or broke, what we now do. The arc earns attention; the pyramid spends it.
- **The memo** — the one-pager: BLUF (bottom line up front), the three supporting facts with their numbers, the risks and what would reverse the recommendation, the ask. The structure of organisations; the rarest and most valuable habit.

The practitioner's rule: **the same analysis, told three ways, or it was never done** — the memo for the record, the pyramid for the room, the arc for the room's attention. Chapter 60's coach-chairman-journalist exercise, professional edition.

## 74.2 The Executive Summary

One paragraph, written last, that contains: the decision it recommends, the single strongest number behind it, the honest uncertainty, and the ask. Test it with Chapter 71's stranger standard: a reader with the relevant job but zero context states the recommendation and its reason after one reading. The summary's discipline is what it *omits* — every method, every caveat that does not change the decision, every number that is not load-bearing. The buried lede (findings on page 14) is the summary written by an analyst protecting themselves; the summary exists to protect the reader.

## 74.3 Charts in the Room

Chapter 17's craft rules, presentation edition:

- **The slide title is the takeaway** — "Churn paused, then resumed: the offer delayed, not prevented", not "Survival curves by cohort". The deck skims as a story if someone reads only the titles — and that is how it will be read.
- **One chart per slide, one message per chart** — the dual-axis monstrosity is two slides hiding in one.
- **The number, named** — every number carries its comparison: "12 minutes" is nothing; "12 minutes, versus 4 last quarter" is a finding.
- **Appendix everything** — the method, the sensitivity tables, the data card: in the deck's back pages, one hyperlink away, shown only when asked (and the asking is a good sign).

**From Your Toolkit — Power BI:** the dashboard is not the deck. The dashboard answers the repeating question; the deck argues the one-time decision — and the commonest craft error is presenting a dashboard walkthrough when the room needs an argument. Pull the one exhibit the argument needs; leave the filters behind.

## 74.4 The Hostile-Question Protocol

The read-out's real work is the Q&A, and it is prepared, not improvised:

1. **Write the three hardest questions** — the ones you hope nobody asks, asked by the smartest sceptic in the room (Chapter 50's students meet them in the capstone; Chapter 61's "anyway" sceptic is their patron).
2. **Answer each in one breath, then stop** — "Good question — the honest answer is we cannot rule that out; here is what we did to bound it" beats a two-minute deflection that concedes the room.
3. **Bridge, don't dodge** — "That's the data question; the decision question it feeds is..." returns the room to the decision without dismissing the asker.
4. **Know your one "I don't know"** — the answer you will give honestly, with a commitment ("by Friday"), because credibility is the asset the Q&A defends — and one honest "I don't know" buys more than three confident guesses.

The protocol's quiet secret: **preparing the hard questions improves the analysis**. The question you cannot answer in one breath is usually the analysis's weak spot — better found in preparation than in the room.

## 74.5 The No-Surprises Rule

Nobody in the decision meeting learns a headline for the first time. The stakeholders who will speak (the sponsor, the objector of Chapter 73's rung four, the budget-holder) have seen the finding in the weekly note or a pre-brief; the meeting decides on a shared understanding instead of staging a reveal. The rule's logic is risk, not manners: surprises trigger status responses (defend, defer, derail) that consume the meeting; shared understanding triggers the decision. The reveal-culture deck is the analyst seeking applause; the no-surprises read-out is the practitioner seeking the signature.

## 74.6 Failure Modes

- **The deck as artefact** — the deck polished past the analysis, the medium become the work; the pyramid test (read only the titles) finds it in minutes.
- **The buried lede** — the finding on page 14, wrapped in method; the executive summary exists to prevent exactly this.
- **The defensive answer** — the two-minute rebuttal that concedes the room; one breath, bridge, or the honest "I don't know".
- **The presentation of the dashboard** — filters in a room that needed an argument; rung discipline again (Chapter 49, 71).

> **Teaching Tip — The three audiences, one analysis:** take one finding (Appendix F plants several) and have students deliver it as the memo, the three-slide pyramid, and the ninety-second arc — then face the hostile-question round (you ask the three hardest). Grading rubric: does each version survive its stranger test, and did any answer run past one breath? Students discover that the constraint is not the analysis but the respect for the audience's minute — which is the entire chapter.

## Key Takeaways

- Three structures, one analysis: memo for the record, pyramid for the room, arc for attention.
- The executive summary: decision, number, uncertainty, ask — and the discipline of omission.
- Slide titles are the story; one chart, one message; the method lives in the appendix.
- Hostile questions are prepared: the three hardest, one breath each, and the honest "I don't know".
- No surprises in the decision room: pre-brief the speakers; the meeting decides, it does not reveal.

## Practice Lab

1. Take Case 1's promotion finding and write it three ways: the one-paragraph memo, the three-slide pyramid, the ninety-second arc. Run the stranger test on each.
2. The title audit: take your last deck and rewrite every title as the takeaway; read only the titles aloud — does the deck tell the story? If not, the slides were labels, not messages.
3. Write the three hardest questions for your capstone (or Case 4's tariff memo); answer each in one breath, on paper, and identify which question exposed a real weakness in the analysis — then fix the analysis.
4. Run the hostile-question round with a partner playing the smartest sceptic; count your breaths; log every defensive answer and rewrite it as bridge-plus-commitment.
5. The pre-brief plan for a fictional read-out: who sees the finding before the meeting, in what form, and what each is asked not to do (reveal, relitigate, reframe).
6. The number audit: list every number in one deliverable; for each, its comparison and what decision it carries — cut the numbers that carry none.
