# Chapter 78: Your First Ninety Days — Quick Wins and the Trust Ledger

*Part XII — The Practitioner's Path: Projects, Clients and the Craft of Delivery*

> "You were hired for your skills; you will be re-hired by your trust balance."

### In this chapter you will learn

- The ninety-day arc: listen, deliver, establish — in that order.
- The listening tour, professional edition: the question ladder as an onboarding instrument.
- The quick win: small, visible, decision-linked, finished — and what it is not.
- The trust ledger: deposits, withdrawals, and the balance you actually manage.
- The first dashboard you do not build, and the metrics you do not volunteer.
- Failure modes: the rookie consultant and the permanent guest.

## 78.1 The Arc

Whether the ninety days are your first job, a new client, or an internal transfer, the arc is the same:

| Days | Phase | The work | The output |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1–30 | Listen | The tour, the map, the reading | The brief for your own first project |
| 31–60 | Deliver | The quick win, done properly | One decision measurably improved |
| 61–90 | Establish | The second project, the systems, the rhythm | The practice's foundations (Chapter 79) |

The arc's discipline is its order: **listen before delivering, deliver before establishing** — and the rookie error is delivering in week one (proving competence) at the cost of listening (learning what competence means here).

## 78.2 The Listening Tour, Onboarding Edition

Chapter 73's ladder, repurposed: six to ten interviews in the first month, each ending in the five questions — plus three that belong to onboarding:

- **"What did the last analyst do that you want kept?"** — the inheritance question (Chapter 72's credit, before any correction).
- **"What analysis exists that nobody trusts?"** — the map of graves; each one is either a fixable lineage problem or a lesson in why it died.
- **"If I fixed one number's credibility, which number?"** — the organisation's pain, in its own words; often the quick win's origin.

The tour's discipline is the write-up: the notes are coded (Chapter 73's SPSS habit), the map is drawn (stakeholders, systems, graves), and the brief for days 31–60 emerges from evidence rather than from whatever the loudest meeting requested.

## 78.3 The Quick Win

Days 31–60 deliver one thing, chosen by four criteria:

1. **Small** — a fortnight's work, not a quarter's; scope that survives contact with the calendar.
2. **Visible** — attached to a decision the organisation knows it makes (the number nobody trusts, fixed; the report that took days, automated honestly).
3. **Decision-linked** — it changes a decision, not a dashboard count (Chapter 1's rule, now yours).
4. **Finished** — delivered with the handover artefacts (Chapter 75), the measurement plan (Chapter 71), and its place in the trust ledger.

The quick win is *not*: the data warehouse rebuild, the ML platform evaluation, the "let's first get all the data clean" project. Those are the second-ninety-days conversations — and the quick win's job is to buy the standing to have them.

## 78.4 The Trust Ledger

The mental model that governs all ninety days: an account per relationship (sponsor, colleague, operator, auditor), with **deposits** (delivered promises, honest "I don't know"s, credit shared publicly, the no-surprises rule kept) and **withdrawals** (missed dates, surprised stakeholders, credit quietly absorbed, defensiveness in reviews).

The ledger's rules:

- **The balance is what you spend** — the second project's scope, the difficult finding's reception, and the refusal (Chapter 72) are all priced from the balance.
- **Deposits compound small** — the Friday five-line note, the fixed number, the public credit: none is dramatic; together they are the practice.
- **Withdrawals are lumpy** — one missed date after three quiet weeks costs more than it seems; the account remembers.
- **Balances are asymmetric by role** — the operator's trust is earned in the trenches (their register respected, their knowledge cited); the sponsor's in the gates (signed briefs, no surprises); audit them separately.

**From Your Toolkit — Excel:** keep the actual ledger — a spreadsheet of relationships, dates, deposits, withdrawals, and the balance read monthly — for the first year. It feels clinical for a month, then becomes the most useful "meeting notes" you have ever kept: the practice's accounts receivable, in the only currency organisations actually pay in.

## 78.5 The Restraint List

What you deliberately do not do in ninety days:

- **The dashboard you were asked for in week one** — "build us a dashboard like the last place's" is a request wearing a solution costume (Chapter 72); the ladder goes up first, and the dashboard that survives the questions is worth ten that precede them.
- **The metrics you volunteer** — the vanity metric offered to seem busy (Chapter 19's discipline, self-applied); every metric you own is a promise to maintain it.
- **The rewrite of the predecessor's work** — Chapter 72's credit-correct-extend; the graveyard analysis often contains the organisation's memory, and the newcomer who burns it spends the next year as the suspect.
- **The opinion on everything** — the first weeks' hot takes are withdrawals with no deposits behind them; the tour's questions are free, its judgements are not.

## 78.6 Failure Modes

- **The rookie consultant** — delivers week one, listens never: competent artefacts answering the wrong questions, a trust ledger overdrawn by the first surprised stakeholder.
- **The permanent guest** — listens forever, delivers nothing: the tour that becomes tourism; day 60 is the quick win's due date, and the calendar is the cure.
- **The win that wasn't visible** — brilliant work on a decision nobody knew they made; the quick win's second criterion (visible) is not optional.
- **The borrowed credit** — the analysis that quietly absorbs the operator's twenty years of knowledge (Chapter 73's knower) and presents it as the analyst's insight; the ledger's fastest withdrawal.

> **Teaching Tip — The trust ledger, graded:** in the final weeks of the course, have students role-play the first ninety days: three stakeholder meetings (one sponsor, one operator, one sceptic) with decisions to make about promises, credit, and a discovered error in the predecessor's work. Grade the ledger, not the script — the write-up of deposits and withdrawals, and the balance's trajectory, is the deliverable. Students report it is the most uncomfortable and most memorable exercise of Part XII: the maths of trust, made visible.

## Key Takeaways

- The arc is ordered: listen (1–30), deliver (31–60), establish (61–90) — and reversing it is the rookie error.
- The onboarding tour adds three questions: what to keep, what is not trusted, which number to fix.
- The quick win is small, visible, decision-linked, and finished — never the platform rebuild.
- The trust ledger is the real account: small compounding deposits, lumpy withdrawals, a balance per relationship, audited monthly.
- The restraint list is as strategic as the to-do list: no premature dashboards, no volunteered metrics, no graveyard-burning, no opinions on everything.

## Practice Lab

1. Write your own ninety-day plan for a real or plausible role: the tour's interview list, the three onboarding questions per interview, the quick-win criteria applied to three candidates, and the chosen one with its fortnight scope.
2. Start the actual trust ledger (the spreadsheet): every stakeholder relationship in your current world, the balance you estimate today, and one deposit scheduled for this week.
3. The quick-win selection drill: take three candidate wins and score each against the four criteria; write the rejection notes for the two you did not choose (they are next quarter's pipeline).
4. The restraint rehearsal: script the week-one dashboard request and your ladder response; practise the sentence that says "yes, in three weeks, after five questions" until it is comfortable.
5. The inheritance memo: for a real predecessor artefact (or Case 3's optimisation), write the credit-correct-extend plan and the paragraph that keeps the operator's knowledge credited.
6. The retrospective: audit a past "first ninety days" (a job, a course, a project) against the arc; write the two withdrawals you would intercept if you could.
