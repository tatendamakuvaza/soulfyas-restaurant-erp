# Chapter 79: Building a Practice — The Service Catalogue

*Part XII — The Practitioner's Path: Projects, Clients and the Craft of Delivery*

> "A job is work you repeat; a practice is work that repeats itself, better each time."

### In this chapter you will learn

- The difference between doing projects and running a practice.
- The service catalogue: defined offers, each with scope, artefacts, and price.
- Repeatability as strategy: templates, checklists, and the asset library.
- The practice dashboard: pipeline, delivery, reuse — measured like anything else.
- Growing the team: juniors, apprenticeship, and the work only seniors should do.
- Failure modes: the bespoke trap and the practice that never systematises.

## 79.1 From Projects to Practice

The project-by-project practitioner reinvents everything quarterly: the scoping conversation from scratch, the pipeline rebuilt per client, the memo format re-decided, the pricing re-guessed. The practice does the opposite — it **systematises what repeats and reserves creativity for what does not**. The instrument of systematisation is the service catalogue: the menu of things the practice does, defined well enough to price, staff, and improve.

The catalogue serves three audiences at once: **clients** (who see a menu of understood offers rather than a mystique of bespoke genius), **the practitioner** (who can scope, staff, and quote from standing definitions), and **the future** (the practice that can be sold, handed over, or scaled — Chapter 80's exit options — because its value is not solely in one head).

## 79.2 The Catalogue

A worked example — the eight services of a small analytics practice (the internal version, for a team inside an organisation, is the same list with internal prices and internal sponsors):

| Service | Scope | Core artefacts | Price shape |
|---|---|---|---|
| Data health audit | 2 weeks | Ten-question findings report (Ch. 76) | Fixed |
| Metric definition & warehouse layer | 3–6 weeks | Contract, models, tests (Ch. 69) | Fixed + retainer |
| Dashboard build | 2–4 weeks | One-audience dashboard + handover (Ch. 18–19) | Fixed |
| Churn / risk model | 4–8 weeks | Model, card, monitoring plan (Ch. 22–25, 43) | Fixed + monitoring retainer |
| Experiment design & analysis | Per test | Design doc, power calc, read-out memo (Ch. 67) | Fixed per test |
| Programme evaluation | 4–12 weeks | Evaluation design, bounded estimate, two-audience memo (Ch. 61, App. J) | Fixed |
| Training & upskilling | Per cohort | Course from this book, assessed (Ch. 50) | Per head |
| Analytics on retainer | Monthly | The question ladder, standing (Ch. 73) | Monthly retainer |

Each service carries a one-page definition: **what it is, what it is not, what the client provides, what the artefacts are, what it costs, what it assumes**. The "what it is not" column is the catalogue's spine — it is Chapter 72's refusal clause, embedded in the menu, so the scope conversation starts from boundaries rather than discovering them in week three.

## 79.3 Repeatability as Strategy

Behind every catalogue line, the asset library — the practice's compounding capital:

- **Templates** — the analysis plan, the options paper, the executive memo, the findings report, the handover memo (Chapters 71–75): each a standing document with the craft already in it.
- **Checklists** — the leakage checklist, the audit's ten questions, the ship-week rubric: the practice's judgement, externalised into artefacts that survive the practitioner's bad week.
- **Pipelines** — the ingestion/validation/transform code (Chapter 69's layer cake) that runs, tested, on the next client's data with configuration rather than rewriting.
- **The case library** — anonymised past engagements, each one a scoping shortcut ("like the co-operative evaluation, but with better records").

The strategic arithmetic: a practice that reuses 60% of its assets on each engagement can quote lower, deliver faster, and price its *novelty* honestly — and every engagement adds assets back. The bespoke practitioner charges for reinventing the wheel; the practice charges for the car.

## 79.4 The Practice Dashboard

The practice measures itself with its own methods (the dashboard eats itself, happily):

- **Pipeline** — enquiries, scoping conversations, options papers out, signed briefs; conversion by service (Chapter 52's funnel, applied to yourself).
- **Delivery** — on-time rate by service, gate slippage (Chapter 71's gates), the review findings that recur (the template's teaching moments).
- **Reuse** — asset-library hit rate per engagement: what percentage of the delivery was standing assets versus new build; the practice's compounding gauge.
- **The value ledger feed** — every engagement's measured outcome (Chapter 80): the dashboard's most important column and the practice's only defensible price list.

**From Your Toolkit — Power BI:** the practice dashboard is the eat-your-own-cooking artefact — cohort curves on client retention, a decomposition tree on revenue by service, the p90 delivery week rather than the mean. Clients who see their consultant's own dashboard trust the craft faster; it is the portfolio that runs.

## 79.5 Growing the Team

The practice's scaling question is who does what, and the answer is apprenticeship-shaped:

- **Juniors do the auditable** — pipelines, validation gates, dashboard builds: work with templates, checklists, and reviews; the apprentice learns the craft where errors are caught (Chapter 50's feedback slot, professional edition).
- **Mid-level does the repeatable complex** — the catalogue's core services, delivered solo, with the senior reviewing the gates.
- **Seniors do the two irreplaceables** — the scoping conversation (the ladder, the refusal, the pricing judgement) and the hardest analysis calls (the sensitivity judgement, the two-audience memo): the work where the experience *is* the asset, and the only work that cannot be templated yet.

The failure to avoid: the practice where the senior does everything (no scale, no succession) and its mirror, the practice where juniors do everything (the brand hollowed out quietly). The catalogue is the answer to both — each service names its staffing shape, and the shape is policy.

## 79.6 Failure Modes

- **The bespoke trap** — every engagement heroic, nothing reused, the practice's revenue capped at the practitioner's calendar; the catalogue is the exit.
- **The never-systematised practice** — busy for years, no assets, unsellable, unscalable; the reuse gauge reads zero and the retirement plan reads "keep working".
- **Catalogue rigidity** — the menu ossified in 2023's methods; Chapter 70's reading discipline is the catalogue's maintenance contract (each service re-priced and re-scoped annually against the grammar's changes).
- **The services nobody buys** — lines kept out of pride; the pipeline data retires them (and the post-mortem — too expensive? badly sold? genuinely unwanted? — feeds Chapter 80's pricing).

> **Teaching Tip — Write the catalogue for your job:** the assignment is one page: your role's eight services, each with its "is not" column and its internal price (hours, or political capital). Students discover their role was always a practice — they were just running it bespoke — and the two services they should retire (nobody values them) and the one they should productise (everybody asks for it) are visible for the first time.

## Key Takeaways

- A practice systematises what repeats: the service catalogue is the menu, the "is not" column is its spine.
- Behind the catalogue, the asset library compounds: templates, checklists, pipelines, cases — reuse is the margin.
- The practice measures itself: pipeline, on-time, reuse, and the value-ledger feed.
- Staffing is policy: juniors on the auditable, mids on the repeatable, seniors on the scoping and the judgement calls.
- Re-price the catalogue annually against the grammar's changes — rigidity is the catalogue's death.

## Practice Lab

1. Write your role's service catalogue: eight lines, each with scope, "is not", artefacts, price shape, and staffing — then mark the two to retire and the one to productise.
2. Start the asset library: list every template, checklist, and pipeline you have built in the last year; score each for reuse potential and pick the three to make standing.
3. Build the practice dashboard (even with one person and three engagements): pipeline, delivery on-time, reuse rate, and the value-ledger column; review it monthly.
4. The staffing plan: for each catalogue service, the junior/mid/senior shape and the review gate; write the apprenticeship path for the junior you will hire or mentor.
5. The pricing review: take one service and re-price it against the last three deliveries' actual effort and the value ledger's outcomes; write the paragraph that justifies the new price to a client.
6. The catalogue audit: score each line against Chapter 70's triage — which service implements changed grammar? Draft next year's version.
