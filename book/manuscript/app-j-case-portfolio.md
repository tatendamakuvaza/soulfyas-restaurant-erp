# Appendix J: Extended Case Portfolio

*Five end-to-end cases for seminar use, capstone inspiration, and interview preparation*

> "Cases are compressed experience: you live a project's hardest week in ninety minutes."

## How to Use These Cases

Each case is written to be attacked, not admired. The recommended seminar format is the case method: students read the case cold (15 minutes), identify the decision and the decision-maker (10 minutes), work the analysis in small groups (30 minutes), then defend their recommendations against challenge (35 minutes). The teaching notes at the end of each case list the traps — the places where the obvious answer is the wrong one.

For interview preparation, use them differently: pick a case, work it alone under a timer, write the one-page memo, and then deliver the executive summary aloud. Chapter 74's structures and Chapter 73's question ladder apply to cases exactly as they apply to live engagements.

The five cases share a world — they can be run in sequence as a single course-long narrative, or independently. All data referenced is generated by Appendix F's Soulfya's generator or by the small generator scripts included in each case; all numbers reconcile across cases, so a cohort can build a shared "institutional memory" of the fictional companies.

## Case 1: The Promotion That Ate the Margin

**Soulfya's Restaurant Group — the Soul Circle double-points promotion**

*Domain: uplift modelling, causal reasoning, experiment design — Chapters 24, 32, 61, 62*

### The Situation

Azu, the founder of Soulfya's, is staring at a report she commissioned and half-regrets. Six months ago, the marketing team ran a double-points promotion through the Soul Circle loyalty programme: members who visited during the promotion window earned twice the usual points. The post-mortem report shows a triumph — visits among promoted members rose 31% versus the prior quarter, and the campaign was hailed internally as the best in the programme's history.

But Azu has two doubts. First, the finance director points out that the promotion's cost (points redeemed, discount processing, campaign administration) rose faster than revenue, and gross margin among promoted members fell 4.2 percentage points. Second, Azu has just read Chapter 61 of this book, and one line is stuck in her head: *the counterfactual is the customer who would have come anyway.*

The loyalty programme has 48,000 members. During the six-week promotion window, 11,200 members visited at least once and received the promotion. The remaining 36,800 members were not targeted — the campaign was sent by email, and only members with an email address on file were eligible. A junior analyst's quick check shows that email-holding members skew higher-income and more urban.

You have member-level data: visit logs for 52 weeks before and 6 weeks after the promotion, redemptions, satisfaction surveys, and a channel flag (email on file or not). You are hired to answer one question: **did the promotion create value, and should it run again?**

### The Work

Your group must produce:

1. **A re-analysis of the 31% lift** — decomposed into what the promotion caused and what would have happened anyway. Email-eligibility selection bias must be addressed; a simple before-after comparison is not acceptable.
2. **An uplift segmentation** — following Chapter 32's four-cell logic: persuadables, sure things, lost causes, sleeping dogs. Estimate the proportion of the 11,200 in each cell (methods: matched controls from the non-email pool, or a model trained on the treated with matched untreated comparators; defend your choice).
3. **A margin-aware value calculation** — the promotion's true incremental profit, using the finance director's numbers: average margin among promoted members fell 4.2 points; points redemption cost averaged $1.85 per redeeming member; campaign administration was $9,000.
4. **A recommendation memo** — one page to Azu: run again, kill, or redesign; if redesign, the targeting rule (which cells to target), the control discipline (how the next run is measured), and the expected value of the redesign.

### The Numbers (for the instructor)

The generator plants: a true treatment effect of +9% visits among persuadables (about 58% of the treated), zero effect among sure things (about 22%), and a small negative effect among sleeping dogs (about 8%, who deferred visits to earn points they then let lapse). The remaining 12% are lost causes. The 31% headline lift decomposes as: +9 percentage points true uplift among persuadables, +18 points of selection bias (email members were visiting more anyway and the pre-quarter was seasonally weak), +4 points of regression to the mean. The honest incremental profit is negative $23,400 — the promotion lost money — but a redesigned campaign targeting only persuadables (identified by pre-period visit recency and frequency) is worth about $31,000 per run with a 10% holdout for measurement.

### Teaching Notes

The traps: (1) the before-after comparison, which every group tries first — let them, then ask what the non-email members did that quarter; (2) averaging uplift across cells — the sleeping dogs' deferral is invisible in the average; (3) forgetting the margin — revenue lift with margin collapse is a well-dressed loss; (4) recommending "run it again but bigger" — the case is designed so that bigger means worse. The strongest groups will ask for the holdout *before* being told it exists, and will design the next campaign as an experiment, not a broadcast.

## Case 2: The Churn Model That Was Too Good

**Soulfya's — the Soul Circle attrition model, second edition**

*Domain: leakage, evaluation honesty, model deployment — Chapters 22, 23, 25, 43*

### The Situation

Six months ago (see Chapter 22's framing), the first churn model went live: gradient boosting on behavioural features, AUC 0.83, used weekly by the retention team to target win-back offers. It has been a quiet success — churn among contacted members is down 1.8 points.

Now the data science team has built version 2. It is dramatically better: AUC 0.97 on the holdout. The team lead wants to swap it into production immediately; the retention manager is thrilled; Azu asks the question that gets you hired: "The model is nearly perfect. What is it seeing that we are not?"

You have: the v2 feature list (47 features), the training data dictionary, the holdout evaluation, and SHAP summary charts showing that three features dominate: `days_since_last_redemption`, `loyalty_status_current`, and `support_tickets_last_30d`.

### The Work

1. **Audit the feature list for leakage** — for each of the 47 features, state the moment in time it becomes available, and whether that moment is before or after the prediction point (the model scores members on Monday to plan the week's outreach). The data dictionary shows `loyalty_status_current` is refreshed nightly, and a member's status becomes "lapsed" 30 days after their last visit — the same event that defines the churn target.
2. **Diagnose the AUC** — explain in plain language why the leak inflates the metric, and estimate the honest AUC by rebuilding the model with the three leaked features removed (provided: the team's rerun, AUC 0.79 — slightly *below* v1).
3. **The decision** — v2 is honestly worse than v1. Write the memo: what ships, what is communicated to the retention team, and what process change prevents v3 from repeating the error (Chapter 43's review gates).
4. **The harder question** — `support_tickets_last_30d` is *not* leaked (tickets precede the prediction point), but it is dominant. The retention manager asks: "Should we stop reading tickets and let the model do it?" Advise on the organisational risk of a model that quietly becomes the only thing anyone reads (Chapter 45's automation complacency).

### Teaching Notes

Traps: (1) groups may accept the "honest" rerun as the fix and miss that v2-without-leaks is *worse than v1* — the decision is not "which model" but "why did we spend three months making a worse model", and the answer is evaluation without baselines; (2) the `days_since_last_redemption` feature is borderline — it is available at prediction time but correlates with the target's definition; a strong group distinguishes *timing* leaks from *definitional* leaks; (3) the automation-compliance question has no clean answer — reward groups that surface it rather than resolve it. The case's quiet lesson: a model that is too good is not lucky, it is lying.

## Case 3: The van in the Wrong Place

**Kunaka Logistics — cold-chain network redesign**

*Domain: optimisation, constraint analysis, simulation — Chapters 47, 67, 71*

### The Situation

Kunaka Logistics runs a 14-vehicle cold-chain distribution network for a dairy processor: one depot, 62 retail drop points, two vehicle classes (5 refrigerated vans, 9 ambient trucks that can carry insulated boxes), and a hard constraint — every drop must arrive within 4 hours of depot departure or the product fails quality control.

The operations manager has a spreadsheet optimisation (built by an intern) that recommends a new route plan projected to cut total distance by 18%. The board has approved the budget contingent on your independent review. The plan looks clean: routes are contiguous, distances check out, vehicle assignments respect capacity. But the drivers' union rep, invited to the review meeting "for completeness", says one sentence that stays with you: "Your plan has us loading the dairy before the pharmaceuticals."

The load sequence matters: pharmaceuticals must be loaded first (strictly controlled temperature ramp), and the plan's most efficient route has the pharma drop scheduled fourth — meaning 90 minutes of door openings before the pharma delivery, which the quality team says risks the temperature excursion limit.

You have: the 62 drop points with coordinates and demand, the current route plan, the intern's optimisation, drive-time matrices (with traffic by hour), the quality standard, and the union rep's account of loading practice.

### The Work

1. **Reproduce the 18%** — verify the optimisation's arithmetic, then test its assumptions: the optimiser minimises distance, not time-under-constraint; rebuild with drive-time and the 4-hour constraint. How much of the 18% survives? (Provided generator: the honest saving is 11%, and two of the optimiser's routes violate the 4-hour window in afternoon traffic.)
2. **Model the loading constraint** — add the pharma-first rule; solve again (a simple heuristic — cluster then sequence — is acceptable and preferred to a black-box solver you cannot explain).
3. **Simulate before you sign** — Monte Carlo the recommended plan: demand ±25%, drive times from the traffic model, one vehicle breakdown per week. What is the plan's service-level distribution? What does the p10 day look like? (Chapter 67's kitchen-sink method.)
4. **The recommendation** — one page to the board: what to approve, what to renegotiate (the intern's plan is not wrong, it is *incomplete*), and what to measure in the first month of operation.

### Teaching Notes

Traps: (1) accepting distance as the objective — the case is engineered so that time-under-constraint reverses two route assignments; (2) the union rep is not an obstruction, he is the constraint's voice — groups that marginalise him miss the case's central lesson (Chapter 73: the person closest to the work knows the model's missing variable); (3) simulation is not optional — the deterministic plan's service level looks fine until the p10 day; (4) the intern's work should be *built on*, not discarded — Chapter 72's options-paper discipline: credit, correct, extend. The board does not need a new optimisation; it needs an honest one.

## Case 4: The Anti-Model

**Harare Metro Water — social tariff design**

*Domain: causal inference, ethics, policy — Chapters 45, 61, 63*

### The Situation

Harare Metro Water serves 1.2 million connections. A cross-subsidy tariff design is on the table: raise the volumetric rate on the top consumption decile to fund a lifeline tariff (first 5 kilolitres free) for the bottom two deciles. The engineering consultancy's model — a sophisticated microsimulation using 2019 consumption data — projects the policy is revenue-neutral and lifts 40,000 households above the water-poverty line.

The city council's data office (your client) has doubts about three things: the 2019 data predates two major tariff shocks that reshaped consumption; the model assumes no behavioural response to price changes (no elasticity); and the lifeline eligibility is based on registered household size, which audit samples suggest is under-reported by about 30% in exactly the households the policy targets.

Your brief is narrow and uncomfortable: **you have two weeks to say whether the model's projection can be trusted, not what the policy should be.**

### The Work

1. **The data audit** — recompute the consumption distribution on post-shock data (provided: a 2023 sample of 40,000 connections). How much does the top decile's share move? (Planted: the top decile's consumption share falls from 34% to 29% — the subsidy base is smaller than projected, and the policy is no longer revenue-neutral; it runs a 6% deficit.)
2. **The elasticity bound** — the model assumes zero behavioural response. Use the two tariff shocks as natural experiments (difference-in-differences against a comparable metro's published data, provided) to estimate a price elasticity range for the top decile; run the policy under your range. (Planted: elasticity around -0.35; under it the deficit widens to 9–11%, because the subsidised decile conserves when priced up.)
3. **The eligibility problem** — model the consequence of 30% under-reporting: how much of the lifeline benefit leaks to households above the poverty line? What is the cost of verification (provided: a doorstep verification pilot cost of $2.10 per household, and a 1.2 million-connection scale)? Is the policy still progressive after verification costs?
4. **The memo** — you were not asked for a recommendation, but Chapter 74's honest-practitioner rule says the analysis implies one. State what the model gets right, what it gets wrong, the bounded range of outcomes, and the decision the council actually faces (fund the deficit, verify eligibility, or redesign). Resist the temptation to decide the politics; do not resist the temptation to be useful.

### Teaching Notes

Traps: (1) groups overstep the brief and become policy advocates — the teaching point is the boundary of the analyst's mandate (Chapter 72's scoping discipline), and the memo's final line should acknowledge it; (2) the elasticity estimate is genuinely uncertain — reward honest bounds over false precision, and expect a DiD table that students struggle to make clean (real natural experiments are messy; that is the lesson); (3) the under-reporting finding cuts *against* the policy's intended beneficiaries, which is uncomfortable — the case tests whether students report unfashionable findings; (4) "two weeks" is real: the deliverable is a bounded, honest memo, not a perfect study. The strongest close: "The model is trustworthy as arithmetic, and misleading as a projection; here is the projection range under defensible assumptions."

## Case 5: The Grant That Would Not Close

**Sable Fields Co-operative — programme evaluation under data loss**

*Domain: data engineering, missing data, survival analysis, integrity — Chapters 11, 15, 63, 75*

### The Situation

Sable Fields is a 2,400-farmer co-operative that took a four-year productivity grant. The funder's close-out report requires an impact evaluation: did grantee farmers' yields and incomes rise more than non-grantees'? The co-operative's manager delivers your data drop with an apology: "The tablets broke in year two. We have complete records for 2021, then nothing until mid-2023, then complete records again."

The dataset: 2,400 farmers (1,100 grantees, 1,300 non-grantees), annual yield and income records, plot sizes, and a participation log. The gap: year 2 (2022) is 78% missing, and — you discover in your first hour — the missingness is not random: farmers with small plots were less likely to be re-enrolled in the record system after the tablet failure, and grantees with small plots were *more* likely to remain recorded (extension officers chased them for compliance photos). The co-operative's board needs the evaluation to succeed for the next grant; the funder's evaluator will pounce on any gap in the data story.

### The Work

1. **Quantify the missingness** — build the missingness table: recorded vs unrecorded by grant status, plot size, and baseline yield. State the mechanism in Rubin's terms (Chapter 11's typology) and defend the classification: this is MAR conditioned on observables, not MNAR — the case is engineered so that the small-plot differential is fully explained by grant status and baseline yield.
2. **Choose and defend a strategy** — multiple imputation (chained equations) versus inverse-probability weighting versus complete-case with bounds. Run the evaluation under your primary strategy and at least one bound; Chapter 15's discipline applies: the sensitivity analysis is the result.
3. **The survival question** — the co-operative also lost farmers: 380 exited the co-operative during the grant period, and exit is correlated with poor yields (you observe exit dates for all 2,400). Naive comparisons of "remaining farmers" overstate impact. Model time-to-exit as a survival curve (Chapter 63), stratified by grant status, and reconcile: does the grant *reduce* exit, or delay it? (Planted: it delays it — the survival curves converge by year 4; the grant's honest effect on yields is +6.1% with CI [2.3, 9.8], and the effect on exit is a delay, not a prevention.)
4. **The close-out memo** — to two audiences at once: the funder's evaluator (technically literate, adversarial) and the co-operative's board (practical, anxious). One document, an executive summary that serves both, and an appendix that pre-empts the evaluator's ten questions (Chapter 76's audit list). Your integrity test: the memo must state the data gap in its first paragraph, not its last.

### Teaching Notes

Traps: (1) complete-case analysis — which the case's construction makes deliver a +14% effect (the planted answer is +6.1%); the naive answer is more than twice the truth, and the board will love it; (2) imputation without the missingness table — the mechanism must be established before the strategy is chosen, or the imputation is decoration; (3) the survival analysis is optional-looking but load-bearing — the exit-delay finding changes the grant's story from "transformation" to "bridging", which is a defensible and fundable finding; (4) the two-audience memo is the hardest deliverable in the case portfolio — most groups write either a technical report or a pep talk, not both in one document. The integrity arc: the data loss is not the co-operative's shame, it is the evaluation's context — and honesty about it is what makes the +6.1% credible enough to fund the next grant.

## The Portfolio's Arc

The five cases are one curriculum. Case 1 teaches that correlation dressed as a campaign report is still correlation. Case 2 teaches that models can lie with perfect accuracy. Case 3 teaches that optimisation without constraints is a plan for a world that does not exist. Case 4 teaches the boundaries of the analyst's mandate. Case 5 teaches that honesty about data's limits is the strongest thing an analyst can publish. Run in sequence, they are a capstone: a student who can work all five — the causal decomposition, the leakage audit, the constrained optimisation with simulation, the bounded policy memo, and the two-audience close-out with missing data — is ready for practice, and has the portfolio to prove it.

The course is complete: the grammar of the craft, the domains where it lives, the frontier that extends it, and the path of delivering it.
