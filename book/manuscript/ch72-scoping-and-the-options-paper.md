# Chapter 72: Scoping and the Options Paper

*Part XII — The Practitioner's Path: Projects, Clients and the Craft of Delivery*

> "The most valuable page you will write is the one that talks the client out of the project."

### In this chapter you will learn

- Scoping as diagnosis: what is actually being asked, and what would answer it.
- The options paper: the do-nothing option, and pricing them all honestly.
- Pricing models: fixed, day-rate, retainer, value — and when each is right.
- The refusal clause: the engagements you decline, on purpose.
- Credit, correct, extend: taking over existing work without burning it.
- Failure modes: the yes-scoped project and the free pilot that never ends.

## 72.1 Scoping Is Diagnosis

Before the project is a project, it is a request — and requests arrive as solutions. "We need a churn model" is a diagnosis the client has already made; the scoping conversation's job is to walk it back to the symptom (win-back spend is rising and conversion is falling), then to the decision (who to call, this week, with what offer), and only then ask whether a model answers it. The tools of the conversation are Chapter 73's question ladder, and its output is the brief Chapter 71 gates. The scoping rule: **you are not selling the analysis; you are diagnosing the decision** — and the analysis is the treatment plan that survives the diagnosis.

## 72.2 The Options Paper

The signature artefact of honest scoping: one page, four options, priced — including the one nobody prices.

| Option | What it is | Cost | Answers the question? | When it is right |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Do nothing | Keep deciding as today | $0 | The status quo, honestly stated | When the decision is rare or low-stakes |
| The quick answer | A day of analysis on existing data | $400 | Often, surprisingly | When the data already holds the answer |
| The full project | The engagement as requested | $4,000 | Thoroughly, with intervals | When the decision recurs or the stakes justify |
| The product | Build the repeating artefact | $15,000 | Forever, if it is maintained | When the question is weekly and the answer is stable |

The do-nothing option is the paper's spine: it prices the status quo's risks and forces the comparison that matters — not "project versus blank", but **project versus the world as it is**. Clients respect the paper that can talk them out of it; and the practitioner who writes it is the practitioner who gets asked back, because the paper proves the advice is not self-serving. Case 3's lesson lives here: the intern's optimisation was *incomplete*, not wrong — the options-paper discipline is credit, correct, extend, with the pricing of each step stated up front.

## 72.3 Pricing the Work

Four models, and the conditions under which each is honest:

- **Fixed price** — when the scope is genuinely known (rare) and the analysis plan is firm. Clear for the client; all the risk sits with you; every scope wobble must re-open the contract, which keeps scope honest.
- **Day rate** — when the work is exploratory and the scope will move. Honest about uncertainty; requires the discipline of the weekly note so the client sees value arriving.
- **Retainer** — when the questions are recurring and the relationship is the product (Chapter 79's service catalogue). Predictable for both; needs an explicit definition of what is *not* included.
- **Value-based** — a share of the measured outcome, when the outcome is measurable and attributable (Chapter 80's value ledger makes this possible). The boldest and the most dangerous: it demands the measurement plan be written before the pricing.

The floor under all four: **your rate is a function of the decision's value, not your effort** — and the options paper is where the client sees that arithmetic, which is why it comes before the pricing conversation, not after.

## 72.4 The Refusal Clause

The engagements you decline, written down so the deciding is policy rather than courage:

- **No decision attached** — "we just want to understand the data better" with no decision, owner, or date: it will eat a quarter and end in a folder.
- **The answer is predetermined** — the analysis is decoration for a choice already made; you are renting your credibility.
- **Unfixable data, unspoken** — when the scoping shows the data cannot answer the question, and the client wants to proceed anyway, the honest refusal is the service.
- **Harm** — the use that fails Chapter 29's harm test (targeting the vulnerable, surveillance, discrimination). Refusing this work is the credential that makes every other engagement trustworthy.

The refusal is delivered with a path: what *would* answer the question, who else could do it, what to fix first. The clause's quiet economics: the engagements you decline are the margin that lets you do the good ones properly.

## 72.5 Taking Over Existing Work

The practitioner inherits: a half-built dashboard, a predecessor's model, the intern's optimisation (Case 3). The discipline that keeps both the work and the relationships: **credit, correct, extend.**

1. **Credit** — name what the prior work got right, in writing, to the people who own it.
2. **Correct** — fix what is wrong as a scoped, visible change, not a silent rewrite.
3. **Extend** — build the new thing on the corrected base, so the lineage (and the credit) survives into the final artefact.

The alternative — the incoming expert's silent rewrite — is how organisations lose their memory every eighteen months, and how consultants earn their reputations for arrogance. The graveyard of prior work is also a graveyard of trust.

## 72.6 Failure Modes

- **The yes-scoped project** — agreement on everything except the decision, metric, and date; six weeks of harmony ending in a disappointed read-out. The gate is the brief's signature, every time.
- **The free pilot that never ends** — the "just show us something" week that becomes an unpaid engagement; the options paper is the vaccine, and the pilot is Option 2, priced.
- **The undersold quick answer** — the day of work that was worth the full price; value-based thinking protects the practitioner's own side of the ledger too.
- **Scope as favours** — each small addition free, the sum of them a different project; re-scope is a conversation, not a confrontation.

> **Teaching Tip — Price the do-nothing:** give students a request (a real one, anonymised) and fifteen minutes to write the options paper — then score the papers on one criterion only: could the client choose Option 1 and feel well served? The papers where do-nothing is a real option, honestly priced, are the papers that would win the work — and students discover that the credibility sale and the honest sale are the same sale.

## Key Takeaways

- Scoping is diagnosis: walk the request back from solution to symptom to decision.
- The options paper prices four worlds, including do-nothing — and its credibility is that it can talk the client out of the project.
- Pricing models are honesty structures: fixed for firm scope, day-rate for moving scope, retainer for recurrence, value for measurability.
- The refusal clause is policy, not courage — and always delivered with a path.
- Inherit by credit, correct, extend: the lineage of work is the lineage of trust.

## Practice Lab

1. Write the options paper for Case 4's tariff brief (the two-week evaluation): all four options, priced, with the do-nothing option's risks stated in the client's own numbers.
2. Take a real request from your organisation; write the scoping conversation's five questions (Chapter 73's ladder) and the brief you expect them to produce.
3. Price the same engagement three ways — fixed, day-rate, value-based — and write the paragraph explaining which you would choose and what each model assumes.
4. Draft your refusal clause: the four declinations, each with its delivered path; rehearse one refusal out loud until it sounds like service rather than rejection.
5. The inheritance drill: take Case 3's intern's optimisation and write the credit-correct-extend memo — three paragraphs, in order, that the board could read aloud.
6. The scope-creep ledger: track one engagement's additions for a month (requested, granted, re-scoped); compute what the "favours" cost at your day rate.
